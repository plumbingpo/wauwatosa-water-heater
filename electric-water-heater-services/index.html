Electric water heaters fail in a few very specific ways, and the symptom usually tells you where to look. No hot water anywhere suggests a tripped breaker, a tripped high-limit reset, or a failed upper element or upper thermostat, because the upper side controls when the lower side gets power. A tank that delivers a few minutes of hot water and then runs lukewarm suggests the lower element or lower thermostat. Water that comes out scalding points to a thermostat stuck closed or an element shorted to the tank, which is also what trips the red reset button. Those are the calls we answer most in Wauwatosa, WI, and nearly all of them are fixable.

Electric water heaters are easy to test when you know what to measure, and that is exactly how Wauwatosa Water Heater starts. At each Wauwatosa home, our technicians measure element resistance, check for elements shorted to the tank, test thermostat operation, confirm the breaker and supply voltage, and inspect every connection from the junction box to the terminals. We also look at sediment. A lower element buried in mineral buildup runs hot, fails early, and can make the tank noisy, so whenever we pull an element, we check how much sediment is sitting on the bottom and flush it out if needed.

The repair itself is usually the easy part. Elements, thermostats, and high-limit switches are standard parts, and our trucks carry the common sizes. Replacing an element means shutting off power, draining the tank, installing the new element, and refilling completely before power is restored, which protects the new element from a dry fire. We also repair damaged wiring, tighten or replace terminal connections, and flush sediment from the tank bottom. If replacement makes more sense, we install new electric heaters sized to your household's first-hour demand, confirm the breaker and wire gauge are correct, and add the expansion tank, drain pan, and strapping your local code requires. How to Tell Something Is Wrong

A heater that is starting to struggle rarely hides it for long, and the clues are easy to spot once you know them. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • A short burst of hot water, then nothing but tepid
  • Cold water everywhere, even after waiting
  • A burning smell or a discolored access panel near the thermostat
  • Water that comes out scalding hot
  • The high-limit reset popping again after you push it
  • Rust-colored hot water or moisture around the heater
  • A circuit breaker for the heater that will not stay on
  • A hissing or sizzling noise near the lower element

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

A typical electric water heater sits on a dedicated 240-volt, 30-amp circuit, which is reason enough not to learn on it. The thermostats sit right next to live terminals behind the access panel, and a meter probe that slips can cause a dangerous arc. Replacing an element also means draining the tank, and forgetting to refill it completely means the new element burns out the moment power returns. Many problems overlap, too. A tripped reset can come from a stuck thermostat, a grounded element, or a loose connection overheating a terminal, so guessing often replaces the wrong part.

Our technicians bring proper meters and a disciplined procedure to every electric heater. They confirm power is off before touching terminals, test element resistance and ground faults, check thermostat operation, and find the reason a high-limit tripped instead of simply pressing it back in. When an element comes out, they drain the tank, check the sediment level, flush if needed, and refill completely before restoring power. You also get an honest opinion on the heater itself. If the tank is leaking or the heater is past about twelve to fifteen years, we will say so before you spend money on a repair.

Simple Ways to Extend Your Water Heater's Life

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Put these technician-approved steps to work at home:

  • Get a diagnosis for any reset button that trips more than once
  • Flush the tank regularly to keep minerals from piling up around the lower element
  • Keep the thermostats set around 120 degrees to save energy and reduce scalding risk
  • Lift the T&P valve lever yearly to make sure it opens and reseats
  • In hard water areas, a softener or low-watt-density elements can extend element life
  • After any draining, refill the tank fully and run a hot faucet before restoring power
  • Ask for an anode rod check every few years, since the anode is what keeps the tank from rusting
